(c) Explain the apparent contradiction

This is a classic example of Simpson's paradox.

Although standard care has a higher recovery rate within each subgroup, the groups are distributed very differently between treatments:

Severity	New treatment	Standard careMild	800	200
Severe	200	800

The new treatment was given mostly to mild patients (who have a high chance of recovery regardless of treatment), while standard care was given mostly to severe patients (who have a much lower chance of recovery).

Because the new-treatment group contains many more mild patients, its overall recovery rate is boosted. Conversely, the standard-care group contains many more severe patients, which depresses its overall recovery rate.

The overall comparison therefore mixes together groups with very different baseline risks and gives a misleading impression.

Which treatment does the data favor?

Since severity is a major prognostic factor and was measured before treatment, the fair comparison is within severity levels (or equivalently after adjusting for severity).

Mild: standard care 90% vs new 80%
Severe: standard care 35% vs new 30%

Thus, the adjusted evidence favors standard care, because it performs better in both severity categories. The overall 70% vs 46% comparison is confounded by the unequal distribution of mild and severe patients across treatments.

Conclusion:

Overall: New treatment appears better (70% vs 46%).
Within both severity groups: Standard care is better.
Explanation: Simpson's paradox caused by unequal severity distributions.
Preferred interpretation: After accounting for severity, the data favor standard care.
