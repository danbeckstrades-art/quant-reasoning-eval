No, the colleague is not correct.

A p-value of 0.03 does not mean "there is a 3% chance the drug has no effect."

What it actually means is:

If the drug truly had no effect (the null hypothesis were true), there would be a 3% probability of obtaining results at least as extreme as those observed purely by chance.

In notation:

p=P(data as extreme or more extreme∣H0 is true)p = P(\text{data as extreme or more extreme} \mid H_0 \text{ is true})p=P(data as extreme or more extreme∣H0​ is true)

where H0H_0H0​ is the hypothesis that the drug has no effect.

The colleague is interpreting the p-value as:

P(H0∣data)P(H_0 \mid \text{data})P(H0​∣data)

which is the probability that the null hypothesis is true given the observed data. A standard p-value does not provide this probability.

Why the distinction matters

The p-value conditions on the assumption that the null hypothesis is true and asks, "How surprising are the data?"

The colleague's statement asks a different question: "Given the data, how likely is it that the null hypothesis is true?"

These are not the same. It is similar to the difference between:

Probability of seeing smoke if there is a fire.
Probability there is a fire if you see smoke.

One does not automatically tell you the other.

Correct interpretation

A correct interpretation would be:

"Assuming the drug truly has no effect, the observed result (or a more extreme one) would occur about 3% of the time by random variation alone."

Since 0.03 is below the common significance level of 0.05, the result would typically be called statistically significant, providing evidence against the null hypothesis. However, it does not tell us the probability that the drug has no effect. Estimating that probability requires additional assumptions and methods, such as a Bayesian analysis.
