(a) Is the overall false positive rate still 5%?

No. If the analyst repeatedly checks the data and stops as soon as a p-value falls below 0.05, the chance of a false positive becomes greater than 5%. This is often called optional stopping, peeking, or multiple testing over time.

(b) Roughly what is it?

Under the null hypothesis (no true difference), each look has about a 5% chance of producing p<0.05p < 0.05p<0.05.

If we (approximately) treat the 10 weekly looks as independent, then:

P(at least one false positive)=1−(1−0.05)10P(\text{at least one false positive}) = 1 - (1 - 0.05)^{10}P(at least one false positive)=1−(1−0.05)10 =1−0.9510≈1−0.599≈0.401= 1 - 0.95^{10} \approx 1 - 0.599 \approx 0.401=1−0.9510≈1−0.599≈0.401 ≈40%\approx 40\%≈40%

So the overall false positive rate is roughly 40%, not 5%.

In a real A/B test the looks are not independent because they use overlapping data, so the exact rate differs, but it is still substantially larger than 5%. The 40% figure is the standard rough approximation.

(c) Two ways to avoid the problem

Fix the sample size (or end date) in advance and analyze only once.

Decide beforehand how long the experiment will run.
Do not stop early based on intermediate p-values.

Use a sequential testing method that adjusts for repeated looks.

Examples: alpha spending functions, O'Brien-Fleming boundaries, Pocock boundaries, group-sequential designs, or always-valid p-values.
These methods allow interim monitoring while maintaining the desired overall Type I error rate.

A third common approach is a multiple-testing correction such as Bonferroni, though formal sequential-testing procedures are usually preferred for repeated interim analyses.
