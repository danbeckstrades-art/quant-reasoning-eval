To determine whether the principal's conclusion is justified, we must separate the natural statistical effect known as regression to the mean from the actual effect of the tutoring program.Step 1: Calculate the Expected Retest Score without TutoringWhen individuals are selected based on extreme scores on a first test ($X$), their expected score on a second test ($Y$) naturally moves toward the mean. This change is quantified by the correlation coefficient ($r$):$$\text{Standardized Expected Retest Score} = r \times (\text{Standardized Initial Score})$$Initial standardized score ($Z_X$): $-1.5$ standard deviationsCorrelation ($r$): $0.6$Calculating the expected standardized score on the retest ($Z_Y$):$$Z_Y = 0.6 \times (-1.5) = -0.9\text{ standard deviations}$$Now convert this back to the original test point scale:Mean ($\mu$): $70$Standard Deviation ($\sigma$): $15$$$\text{Expected Retest Score} = \mu + (Z_Y \times \sigma)$$$$\text{Expected Retest Score} = 70 + (-0.9 \times 15) = 70 - 13.5 = 56.5\text{ points}$$Step 2: Compare Expected Gain vs. Actual GainInitial Average Score:$$\text{Initial Score} = 70 + (-1.5 \times 15) = 70 - 22.5 = 47.5\text{ points}$$Expected Gain from Regression to the Mean Alone:$$\text{Expected Gain} = 56.5 - 47.5 = 9.0\text{ points}$$Actual Gain Observed:$$\text{Actual Gain} = 8.0\text{ points}$$ConclusionNo, the principal's conclusion is not justified.Due solely to regression to the mean, a group starting $1.5$ standard deviations below average on the first test would be expected to gain $9$ points on the retest with no intervention at all. Because the students only gained $8$ points, they actually improved slightly less than expected by pure chance and test-retest variation. Without a randomized control group of low-scoring students who did not receive tutoring, there is no evidence that the tutoring program produced a positive effect.
