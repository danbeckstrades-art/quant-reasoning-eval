(a) What is wrong with the two-sample t-test approachViolation of Independence: An independent (two-sample) t-test assumes that observations in Group 1 and Group 2 are independent of each other. Because the measurements come from the exact same 30 users, the before and after scores are paired and naturally correlated.Loss of Statistical Power: Treating paired data as independent ignores the within-subject correlation. This includes unnecessary between-subject variability in the pooled variance calculation, which artificially increases standard error and reduces the power to detect a true effect.(b) Correct Test: Paired t-TestTo test whether the mean difference is significantly different from zero ($H_0: \mu_d = 0$ vs. $H_a: \mu_d \neq 0$):1. Calculate the Standard Error ($SE$)$$SE = \frac{s_d}{\sqrt{n}} = \frac{5.0}{\sqrt{30}} \approx \frac{5.0}{5.4772} \approx 0.9129$$2. Compute the t-Statistic$$t = \frac{\bar{d} - 0}{SE} = \frac{2.0}{0.9129} \approx 2.191$$3. DecisionCalculated $t$-statistic: $2.191$Critical value ($df = 29, \alpha = 0.05$, two-tailed): $2.045$Because $\vert{}2.191\vert{} > 2.045$, the result rejects the null hypothesis at the 5% significance level. There is a statistically significant difference in performance before and after the redesign.
