Let's evaluate each statement step-by-step using standard frequentist statistical principles.(a) "There is a 95% probability that the true difference lies between 0.3 and 2.1."Incorrect.In frequentist statistics, the true difference between means ($\mu_T - \mu_C$) is a fixed, unknown constant, not a random variable. Once the specific interval $(0.3, 2.1)$ has been calculated from the observed sample data, it either contains the true parameter or it does not—the probability is strictly either $1$ or $0$. The $95\%$ probability refers to the long-run success rate of the procedure used to generate the interval, not to this single, fixed numerical interval.(Note: Expressing this as a probability statement about the parameter itself is a Bayesian interpretation, which requires a prior distribution and yields a Bayesian credible interval rather than a frequentist confidence interval).(b) "If the study were repeated many times, about 95% of the intervals constructed this way would contain the true difference."Correct.This is the precise definition of a frequentist confidence interval. It describes the long-run performance of the estimation method under repeated sampling from the same population: if we draw repeated random samples of the same size and construct a $95\%$ confidence interval for each sample, approximately $95\%$ of those calculated intervals will capture the true population mean difference.(c) "95% of individual patients have outcome differences between 0.3 and 2.1."Incorrect.A confidence interval estimates a population parameter (the difference in average/mean outcomes across groups), not the variability of individual data points. Individual patient outcomes vary much more widely than the mean of a group (due to standard deviation vs. standard error). To capture where a specified proportion of individual patient values fall, one would calculate a prediction interval or a tolerance interval, which would be significantly wider than $(0.3, 2.1)$.(d) "A two-sided test of 'no difference' at the 5% level would reject."Incorrect.There is a direct mathematical duality between a $95\%$ confidence interval and a two-sided hypothesis test at the $\alpha = 0.05$ significance level:The null hypothesis of "no difference" corresponds to a mean difference of $0$ ($H_0: \mu_T - \mu_C = 0$).A two-sided hypothesis test at $\alpha = 0.05$ rejects $H_0$ if and only if the null value ($0$) falls outside the $95\%$ confidence interval.Here, the interval $(0.3, 2.1)$ consists entirely of positive numbers and does not contain $0$.Therefore, the test would reject the null hypothesis of no difference at the 5% significance level (or $p < 0.05$).
